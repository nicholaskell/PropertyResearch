# Equine / Horse Boarding

Evaluate whether a property could support a practical, insurable and locally marketable horse-boarding operation. This is an **optional income opportunity**, not a purchase requirement. A property without equine infrastructure should not lose points in the household/person scores; useful existing infrastructure can earn bonus value.

Start with a lower-labor **pasture-board or pasture/full-care hybrid** model. Evaluate stall/full-care or training-board models only when the property, market and likely staffing support them.

## Customer Demand Research

For every property, estimate **Customer Demand Likelihood: VERY LOW / LOW / MODERATE / HIGH / VERY HIGH / UNKNOWN** and **Demand Confidence: LOW / MEDIUM / HIGH**.

Do not infer demand merely from a rural setting, acreage, “horse country” reputation, or the presence of a barn. Research the realistic service area—normally 20–30 minutes for routine boarding, extended to 45 minutes only for differentiated facilities or sparse rural markets—and report:

- Local horse/equine population proxies: USDA Census of Agriculture county horse/pony inventory and farms, state equine data, horse-property density, shows, clubs, 4-H, trail systems, veterinarians, farriers, feed/tack stores and trainers.
- Demand-base proxies: households and income within the service radius, population/household direction, affluent exurban growth, and commute access to meaningful population centers.
- Direct competition: active boarding barns and advertised openings/waitlists when visible; drive time, board type, capacity, amenities, current prices, reviews/activity and evidence that the business is still operating.
- Market gap: pasture versus stall/full-care supply, indoor/outdoor arena, trail access, trailer storage, retirement/layup, training/lessons and other underserved niches.
- Friction: winter climate/grazing season, hay/feed availability, distance from owners, poor roads/trailer access, weak incomes, declining population, saturated competition or unusually low local prices.
- Evidence limits: public directories and social media can omit small barns or retain closed businesses. Label estimated competitor counts and do not treat “no results” as “no competition.”

When evidence permits, include a conservative estimate of likely fill:

- **Estimated feasible capacity** based on usable land, facilities, local rules and management model.
- **Likely occupied horses at stabilization** as a range, not a precise promise.
- **Expected time to stabilization** and the assumptions behind it.
- A downside case at 50% occupancy.
- The exact validation step before relying on demand: calls to local vets/farriers/feed stores, competitor availability/price calls, local horse-group inquiry, or a small pre-marketing/waitlist test.

## Operating Model & Economics

State the likely model: pasture/self-care, pasture/full-care, stall/self-care, full-care, or premium/training. Keep owner labor separate from operating profit.

Report ranges for:

- Monthly rate per horse and realistic occupied capacity.
- Stabilized annual gross revenue.
- Hay/forage, feed/supplements, bedding, pasture inputs, fence/barn maintenance, water/electricity, commercial/equine insurance, manure/grounds, advertising/admin and hired relief labor.
- Annual operating cost and operating income **before owner labor, debt service, tax and depreciation**.
- Estimated owner hours and the cost of dependable 365-day coverage.
- Break-even occupied horses and 50%/75%/90% occupancy scenarios.
- Startup/capital needs: perimeter and cross fencing, gates/paddocks, shelters, water, barn/stall conversion, gravel/parking, arena and code/access work.
- Replacement value of existing useful equine infrastructure.
- Income per operational acre and comparison with other plausible property uses.

Use local prices when available. For early screening only, clearly label broad planning assumptions; do not copy national revenue ranges into a local conclusion without adjustment.

## Property Requirements / Risks

Verify or flag:

- Commercial boarding, lessons/training, events and signage under zoning; animal-unit, setback, manure, parking and home-occupation rules.
- Commercial/equine liability and property-insurance availability.
- Pasture condition, drainage, shade, safe fencing, water reliability, shelter and manure handling.
- All-weather horse-trailer route, entrance width, sight distance, parking and turnaround.
- Separation from the residence, dogs, roads, neighbors and firewood/sawmill operations.
- Whether improvements remain useful for livestock, equipment/hay storage or resale if boarding demand disappoints.
