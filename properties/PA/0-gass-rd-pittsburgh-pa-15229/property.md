## Gating Issue #1 — Topography / Landslide / Grading

This is the most important unresolved physical risk.

The listing aerial clearly shows dense woodland on a parcel lying between established streets at different elevations. The image is consistent with a hillside parcel, but slope percentages have **not yet been established from parcel-specific LiDAR/topographic contours**.

Ross Township has unusually consequential steep-slope rules. A subdivision/land-development applicant must provide a five-foot-contour topographic site plan and classify slopes. Maximum disturbance is approximately:

- 12–14.9% slope: 40% of area, or 30% in landslide-prone area
- 15–25% slope: 30%, or 20% in landslide-prone area
- >25% slope: 15%, or 5% in landslide-prone area

Within a landslide-prone area, structures, roads and vehicular-use areas cannot be within 20 feet of a slope greater than 30%.

Ross also specifically requires developers to evaluate moderate/high landslide susceptibility. Southwestern Pennsylvania hillside geology makes this a real underwriting issue, not a theoretical footnote.

### Interpretation

If most of the road frontage contains buildable benches at <12% slope, a 4–6 lot frontage plan could remain attractive.

If substantial portions of the frontage/building envelopes are 15–25%+ or mapped landslide-prone, grading/retaining/stormwater cost can rapidly erase the lot-margin thesis.

**Fatal-flaw classification: TOPOGRAPHY / LANDSLIDE = INVESTIGATE.** No fail established yet.

## Gating Issue #2 — Gass Road Access / PennDOT

Ross Township's official street classification lists Gass Road as **state-owned**. Depending on segment it is classified as a Principal Arterial or Urban Collector.

New or altered driveways/local roads connecting to a state highway require a PennDOT Highway Occupancy Permit (HOP). PennDOT evaluates access location, sight distance, drainage, safety and highway operations.

This is particularly important for a subdivision because PennDOT's own HOP manual allows an **Access Covenant** when future subdivision could produce an unacceptable number or location of driveways. Such a covenant can restrict future access to approved locations.

Therefore, the concept cannot assume five or six independent curb cuts. PennDOT could require fewer shared accesses or a common/internal road arrangement.

### Interpretation

A four-to-six-lot plan remains geometrically plausible, but **road-access feasibility must be tested before assigning value to hypothetical lots**.

**Fatal-flaw classification: LEGAL/PRACTICAL ACCESS = INVESTIGATE.**
